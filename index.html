Who's Really Watching?

VPN use, censorship, and trust in government among young people in Kazakhstan.

For five days in January 2022, Kazakhstan went dark — WhatsApp, Telegram, and Signal blocked first, then the entire internet. Some people waited it out. Some didn't.

This is about the ones who found a way through.

Jester · PLS 210 · Fall 2026

01 — RESEARCH QUESTION

The Question

In January 2022, Kazakhstan blocked WhatsApp, Telegram, and Signal, then cut off the internet nationwide for roughly five days during the unrest.

To what extent does VPN use predict lower trust in government among young people in Kazakhstan?

The gap between waiting and circumventing is what this project tests.

02 — EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITIONING

Why It Fits

VPN use and government trust are treated as real, measurable things — not meanings to interpret. The hypothesis is stated before any data is collected, and it could turn out to be wrong. That falsifiability is the commitment.

Independent Variable
VPN use
Dependent Variable
Trust in government
02 — EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITIONING

We Are Positivist

This is not an inquiry into how people narrate or make sense of censorship. It does not start from an open question and build meaning inductively from individual stories.

It starts from a hypothesis, names its variables in advance, and asks whether they move together in a way that could, in principle, be proven wrong. That commitment to a testable, falsifiable claim is what makes this positivist.

02 — EPISTEMOLOGICAL POSITIONING

What I Am Not Doing

  • Not claiming proof of causation from a single cross-sectional design
  • Not measuring what the government censors — only whether bypassing it relates to trust
03 — CONTRIBUTION

My Contribution

This exact question — does bypassing censorship predict lower trust — has already been tested in China and Ukraine.

Nobody has tested it in Kazakhstan.

Despite Kazakhstan having one of the most severe, best-documented internet shutdowns on record. Not a new theory — an existing one, tested on a case nobody's checked.

04 — LITERATURE REVIEW / SOURCE 1

Chen & Yang (2018)

A field experiment randomly assigned 1,800 university students in Beijing to either restricted internet or free, uncensored access, then tracked them for 18 months. Free access alone didn't make students seek out politically sensitive content — but once they acquired it, it produced broad, lasting shifts in knowledge, beliefs, and political attitudes, including greater skepticism of government performance.
— Chen & Yang, "The Impact of Media Censorship: Evidence from a Field Experiment in China" (2018)

This is the causal backbone: randomized access, not just correlation.

04 — LITERATURE REVIEW / SOURCE 2

Mou, Wu, & Atkin (2016)

A survey of 424 Chinese circumvention-tool users found that of all the macro-social factors tested, political trust was one of the only ones that significantly predicted use — lower trust, more frequent VPN use.
— Mou, Wu, & Atkin, "Understanding the Use of Circumvention Tools to Bypass Online Censorship," New Media & Society, 18(5) (2016)

Same direction as the hypothesis — this time from survey data, not an experiment.

04 — LITERATURE REVIEW / SOURCE 3

Golovchenko (forthcoming)

When Ukraine blocked VKontakte, even a small technical hassle — a few extra clicks for a VPN — cut usage roughly in half. Circumventing the block tracked users' political alignment, not just their comfort with technology.
— Golovchenko, "Fighting Propaganda With Censorship," Journal of Politics (forthcoming); as reported in Lewis, Boston Globe, 2022

Circumvention itself carries political meaning — it isn't just a workaround.

04 — LITERATURE REVIEW / GAP

Nobody has asked this in Kazakhstan.

China and Ukraine are studied. A five-day, near-total national shutdown is not.

05 — THE PROBLEM
If circumventing censorship is risky and inconvenient, why do people bother at all?
MY ANSWER, FROM THE READING

Exposure to uncensored information persistently increased students' trust in foreign sources and their willingness to pay to keep that access — meaning the value of circumventing rises once people experience what's on the other side, even though the act itself carries risk and effort (Chen & Yang, 2018).

06 — WHERE THE REAL TENSION IS

Causal vs. Correlational

Causal

Chen & Yang can claim causation — access was randomly assigned. Circumvention changed beliefs, not the other way around.

Correlational

Mou et al. and Golovchenko can't rule out reverse causation — people who already distrust the government may simply be the ones who bother to circumvent.

This project's own design will sit on the correlational side. That's a real limitation, named honestly.
